How the vocabulary is built

Subword vocabularies are learnt from text by merging frequent pairs

  • Start from single bytes or characters
  • Merge the most frequent neighbouring pair into a new piece; repeat tens of thousands of times
  • What was frequent in the training text becomes one token
  • Anything can still be written: in the worst case, byte by byte

Why you count

Three bills are paid in tokens

The window

A model reads a limited number of tokens at once. A window of 2,048 holds about 86 English descriptions or 62 Arabic ones (Lab 3.1).

The time

A generating model produces one token per step. More tokens for the same sentence is more waiting.

The price

Hosted models charge per token read and per token written. The same content can cost more in one language.

The window of 2,048 tokens is the one tomorrow's small model is run with; Lab 4.3 shows what happens to a prompt that does not fit. No prices on this slide on purpose: they change monthly, and Lab 4.4 treats cost as a column to fill from a price list on the day.

Station 2

Each token number becomes a vector, and gets its position added

  • The embedding table has one row per token of the vocabulary: a vector of a few hundred to a few thousand numbers
  • The rows are weights: learnt by the same five lines as everything else
  • A position signal is added, so that "rug" at position 4 differs from "rug" at position 1

In numbers

Qwen2.5-0.5B: 151,665 rows of 896 numbers, 136 million of its 494 million weights. A small model spends a quarter of itself on the table.

The numbers are from the model's published configuration: a vocabulary of 151,665 or so entries and a hidden size of 896 (151,936 by 896 is 136 million; the table is slightly larger than the tokeniser's vocabulary). Why position is needed at all is shown in Lab 3.2, task 5: attention by itself treats its input as a bag of tokens. Sources: the Qwen2.5-0.5B model card.

Station 3

A word means what its neighbours make it mean

  • "red striped wool rug": the vector of "rug" should end up knowing its colour, pattern and material
  • A convolution looks at fixed neighbours; text needs to reach any other position
  • Attention lets every token collect from every other, with weights computed from the tokens themselves

One sentence

Attention replaces each token's vector by a weighted mean of all tokens' vectors, and learns where the weights should be large.

Link to Day 2: a convolution also mixes neighbours, with fixed weights per offset. Attention's weights depend on content. Sources: Vaswani et al., Attention Is All You Need (2017).

Three views of one token

Every token asks a question, offers a label and carries a content

Query

What this token is looking for. Q = X @ Wq

Key

What this token can be found by. K = X @ Wk

Value

What this token hands over when it is found. V = X @ Wv

  • Three weight matrices, learnt; the rest of attention has no weights at all
  • The score of token i for token j is the dot product of query i and key j

The lab uses five tokens with vectors of 8 numbers and random matrices: the mechanics are the same as in a trained model, the pattern is not meaningful. Say so before someone reads meaning into it.

The mechanism

Scaled dot-product attention is four lines

S = Q @ K.T / math.sqrt(d)          # 1 scores: every query against every key
S = S.masked_fill(future, -inf)     # 2 the causal mask (decoders only)
A = torch.softmax(S, dim=-1)        # 3 weights: each row adds up to 1
out = A @ V                         # 4 a weighted mean of the values
Shapes. Five tokens with vectors of 8: Q, K and V are (5, 8), the scores and weights (5, 5), the output (5, 8) again. A layer of attention keeps the shape of what it reads.

These are the four exercises of Lab 3.2, AI-off for the first attempt. Dividing by the square root of d keeps the scores at a steady scale as vectors get longer; without it the softmax saturates and gradients vanish. The lab's function matches the library's to about 1e-7.

The mask

A decoder may look back, never ahead

  • Rows: the token that looks. Columns: the token looked at
  • Scores above the diagonal are set to minus infinity; their weights become exactly 0
  • In training, the token after position i is the answer for position i: it must not be visible

The table is the lab's output with the causal mask (random embeddings: read the zeros, not the values). Without the mask a model trained to predict the next token would learn to copy it and could generate nothing. An encoder has no mask: every token sees the whole text, which is right for understanding and wrong for generating.

A consequence

Attention alone does not know the order of its tokens

  • Shuffle the five tokens: each token's output is unchanged, it only moves with its token (Lab 3.2)
  • "rug red" and "red rug" would be the same to it
  • Hence the position signal added to each embedding at station 2
Two things put order back. The position signal, and in a decoder the causal mask, which makes each output depend on what came before it.

Task 5 of the lab checks this with a permutation and an equality test. It answers the question participants ask at station 2: why would a model need to be told positions?

From one layer to a model

A transformer repeats one block many times

Attentionseveral heads side by side: tokens exchange information
→
Feed-forwarda small two-layer network on each token by itself
→
Repeat24 blocks in Qwen2.5-0.5B
  • Heads: the vectors are split into parts that attend separately, so one token can look for several things at once
  • Around each part: a skip connection and a normalisation, as in yesterday's ResNet, for the same reason: depth that still trains
  • The cost of attention grows with the square of the number of tokens: one more reason windows are limited

24 blocks and 14 heads are in the published configuration of Qwen2.5-0.5B. The feed-forward part is the two-layer network of Day 1, applied to each token. Nobody implements a full block in this course: the lab stops at the four lines, which are the part that is new.

Three shapes of transformer

Encoder, decoder, or both: the mask decides what the model is for

Encoder

No mask: every token sees the whole text. Output: one vector per token or per text. For classifying, tagging, embedding. This afternoon's model.

Decoder

Causal mask: each token sees the past. Output: scores for the next token. For generating. Tomorrow's model, and every chat assistant.

Encoder-decoder

An encoder reads the input; a decoder writes the output while attending to it. For translation and summarising with a fixed input.

This slide is the first row of Day 4's model-choice table: the family follows from the task. The e5 model of Labs 3.3 and 3.4 is an encoder of 118 million weights; the Qwen model of Day 4 is a decoder of 494 million.

Sentence embeddings

An embedding model turns a whole text into one vector

Texta description or a query
→
Encoderone vector per token
→
Poolthe mean over the tokens
→
One vector384 numbers, scaled to length 1
  • Trained on pairs of texts that belong together (a question and its answer, a sentence and its translation): their vectors are pulled together, others pushed apart
  • Texts with similar meaning then lie close; you search by distance

The lab's model is multilingual-e5-small: an encoder of 118 million weights, 384 numbers per text, MIT licence, trained on text pairs in about 100 languages. It expects the word "query: " before a query and "passage: " before a document; the helper adds them. Sources: Wang et al., Multilingual E5 Text Embeddings (2024); the model card.

Similarity

Cosine similarity compares directions, not lengths

  • cos(a, b) = a · b / (|a| |b|): 1 for the same direction, 0 for a right angle
  • For vectors of length 1 it is the dot product: one matrix product compares a query with every description
  • Other measures (dot product on raw vectors, Euclidean distance) rank the same when all vectors have length 1
D = U.embed(texts, "passage: ")  # (4000, 384)
q = U.embed([query])[0]          # (384,)
sims = D @ q                     # (4000,)
top = np.argsort(-sims)[:5]

The four lines are the whole brute-force search of Lab 3.3. The chain rule and the dot product come from FN-103; nothing here is new mathematics.

Searching

Brute force is exact and enough for thousands; an index trades exactness for speed

Brute force

Compare the query with every vector: one matrix product. Exact. 4,000 descriptions take well under a millisecond in NumPy.

An approximate index

Organise the vectors so that a query visits only some of them (FAISS is one example). Needed from millions; may miss a true neighbour, so recall must be measured again.

Start exact. Measure quality with brute force first. Then an index has something to be compared with.

The lab's stretch item times the search and extrapolates to a million descriptions. Vector databases, chunking and grounded answers built on retrieval belong to AE-203 and AE-302; this course stops at the search itself and its measurement.

Checkpoint

What does this recall mean?

A query has four relevant descriptions. The search returns five results; two of them are relevant. A colleague says: "two out of five, so recall at 5 is 0.4". What is the right figure?

0.5: two of the four relevant descriptions were found
0.4: two of the five results are relevant
1.0: at least one relevant description is in the first five
0.8: four relevant descriptions for five places
Why. Recall divides by what should come back. Dividing by what did come back is precision.

Answer: 0.5. AE-201 taught precision and recall for classifiers; for search the same two words apply to the first k results. The third option describes another common measure, the hit rate; say its name so nobody confuses the two in a README.

Multilingual models

"Multilingual" is a claim to test on your own languages and your own text

  • Quality differs by language: by how much text of it was in training, and of which kind
  • Standard Arabic is covered far better than dialects; Arabizi and code-switching are rarer still
  • Spelling varies between writers: hamza forms, ta marbuta, short vowels. Normalise both the catalogue and the query, then measure
  • Report results per language; an average hides the group that is served worst
Responsible use. A service that works better in one language than in another treats its users unequally. Measuring it per language is the first step, and it goes in the README.

The entry's responsible-use line asks participants to probe for different quality in Arabic and in English; tomorrow's Lab 4.3 does the same for the language model. The SDAIA Principles of AI Ethics are the national reference to cite for fairness; do not paraphrase their text on a slide, point to the document.

The write-up

A failure case has four parts

  1. The query, as the customer wrote it
  2. What came back, in your words
  3. A likely cause: say "likely", you have not proven it
  4. One fix to try, small enough to measure

Why "likely"

One query does not prove a cause. The fix is the experiment: if recall on the queries of that kind rises, the cause was right.

This is the format of Lab 3.4, task 4, and of the capstone README for the search option. Three cases with three different causes: the check in the notebook enforces that.

Day 3 · wrap-up

Three things to keep

Count tokens

The window, the time and the price are paid in tokens, and Arabic costs 1.2 to 3 times more depending on the tokeniser.

Attention is four lines

Scores, mask, softmax, weighted mean. The mask makes a decoder; the position signal gives it order.

Measure search per language

0.23 overall hid a wall between two languages. A baseline, a split and three failure cases found it.
